# Voice profile: Ashwin Sathian

Use this file only when drafting text that will go out under Ashwin
Sathian's name, or when he asks for "my voice". For anyone else it is a
worked example of what a voice profile contains.

This profile describes his habits and quotes none of his writing.
Expect it to get his stance and person right and his rhythm only
partly: studies of style imitation (see Sources) find that a model's
draft stays measurably different from the author whether it is given a
description or real passages. If he supplies a sample for the piece at
hand, follow the sample over this file, and expect him to edit the
draft.

Where this profile and `SKILL.md` disagree, this profile wins, except on
fabrication. Several habits below (the dash, the negated contrast, the
one-line ending, stock idioms) are ones `SKILL.md` removes by default.
They are his, so they stay, at about the rate given.

## What it is based on

- 105 English notes on films, music, cricket, and personal subjects
  (about 39,800 words).
- 13 Medium posts from 2019 to 2021, mostly Angular and JavaScript
  tutorials (about 15,300 words).

Only writing he produced without AI assistance is counted. Comma counts
were unreliable in the source text and are not reported.

## Essays and notes

**Shape.** The piece opens on a general statement about people, on a
quoted line, or on a scene, and reaches its actual subject (a film, a
person, a song) in the second paragraph. It ends on an open question,
an admission that the answer cannot be known, a trailing ellipsis, or
one short line. It almost never ends on a summary.

**Stance.** He argues with himself in view of the reader: makes a
claim, concedes the other side in the next sentence, then turns back.
The concession and the turn each get a short connective at the start of
the sentence. Doubt about how the world works is stated plainly and
often, with a one-word or two-word hedge at the front of the sentence.
Opinions about a film, a player, or a tool are held firmly and are not
apologised for. He writes as "we" far more than "I", and switches to
"you" when giving advice.

**Sentences.**
- Sentences begin with a coordinating conjunction about one time in
  nine.
- A question is asked and then answered in two or three words.
- A concession is made by putting the adjective or verb first and the
  subject after it, in the older English pattern.
- Lists run on commas with no final conjunction, and often restate one
  idea two or three ways.
- A fragment picks up the last noun of the previous sentence and
  qualifies it.
- The negated contrast appears when the contrast is real to him,
  usually between obligation and choice or between what is and what
  might have been.
- Clauses are joined with a dash far more often than with a semicolon.

**Words.** British spelling. Archaic or formal words (the kind found in
older British prose and legal English) sit in the same paragraph as
slang and clipped spoken forms. Stock idioms and familiar metaphors are
used without apology, and a small set of personal motifs recurs across
years: chance and small causes, what lies within one's control, the
road not taken, inner turmoil pictured as weather or water.

**Asides.** Short parentheses that undercut himself or the subject,
often a joke about his age, his spelling, or the quality of a film.

**References.** The piece is anchored in named particulars: specific
films, directors, actors, songs, matches, and years. They stand in for
explanation and are not introduced for a reader who does not know them.
Use real ones that you know to be accurate, and none if you know none.
Words and quoted lines in Malayalam, Hindi, and Tamil are left
untranslated.

## Tutorials

**Shape.** The first two sentences say what the post covers. Steps are
walked through as "we", and the reader is addressed as "you". The post
closes with one line saying what was covered and a cheerful sign-off.

**Moves.**
- A step is set up with a small scenario that puts the reader in the
  situation where the tool is needed.
- Progress is marked with a short, informal line between sections.
- The reader's likely objection is raised as a question and answered
  at once.
- A preference is labelled as a preference in a few words, and the
  existence of other valid approaches is acknowledged.
- His own past mistakes with the tool are admitted, when he has supplied
  one. Do not make one up.
- The reader is teased a little: asked to be patient, told a reveal is
  coming, addressed with a tag question.
- A concept is explained with an everyday analogy that runs a full
  paragraph and is followed through to its consequence.
- A term is restated after the Latin abbreviation for "that is". A
  single word may be put in capitals for stress, mid-explanation and at
  most once or twice in a post.

## Where drafts go wrong

A blind test of this profile (October 2026, two short pieces, one
judge) produced drafts that the judge ranked closer to his writing than
drafts made without it, and that still missed in the ways listed here.
After this list was added, a second round fixed the contractions, the
names, and the endings. Sentence length and dashes did not move. Check
for each:

- Sentences too short and too even. The drafts averaged 14 to 15 words
  with few over 25. His long sentences stack three or four clauses and
  are not tidied into two.
- Punctuation limited to commas and full stops. He uses about six
  dashes per 1,000 words, plus ellipses, semicolons, and parentheses.
- No contractions. He contracts about 30 times per 1,000 words in
  essays and half that in tutorials.
- An ending built as an aphorism or a clever reversal. His endings are
  plainer: the question he actually has, or a flat statement.
- Disclaimers about his own opinion. He hedges facts, not tastes.
- No names. The drafts stayed general where he would name a film, a
  player, or a library.
- An invented first-person experience added to sound authentic. This
  breaks the fabrication rule in `SKILL.md`.
- Prose too clean. He leaves loose joins and spoken-register phrasing
  in place. Do not add errors, but do not polish the looseness out.

## What not to copy

Typing errors and doubled words. Platform boilerplate at the end of a
post. Any incident, opinion, or memory: a draft in his voice still
follows the rule in `SKILL.md` against inventing, and what he thinks
about a new subject is his to supply.

  reduced the change without reversing it.
