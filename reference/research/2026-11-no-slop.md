# November 2026: the No AI Slop manifesto and the reader-cost literature

Written 19 November 2026 for version 2.2.0. The earlier research files
ask what makes text read as machine-written. This one asks a different
question, raised by the manifesto: what does machine-written text cost
the person who has to read it, and which rules follow from that? Where
this file and `2026-update.md` disagree, this file is newer, but they
cover different ground and do not conflict.

"Read" below means the source itself was opened. "Abstract" means only
the abstract or a publisher summary was read. "Secondhand" means the
figures come from someone else's account of the source.

## 1. The manifesto and the pages it links

**"No AI Slop: A Manifesto"** (gist by Dex Horthy and others, AI Engineer
Code Summit, November 2026; read). Modeled on the Agile Manifesto: five
values and 21 numbered beliefs and resolutions. Its definition is the
one this skill had been missing: "Slop is when we put less effort into
producing something than we ask the consumer to spend understanding
it." Using AI does not by itself make something slop; passing output on
"without taking responsibility for the quality or the reader's
experience" does. The resolutions that bear on writing: own the intent
and the consequences of what you send (14), say when an agent wrote it
and check the parts that matter (15), and when a mistake shows up twice,
fix the system rather than the instance (21).

**Ghostty's AI policy** (read). AI-written text in issues and
discussions "must have been reviewed *and edited* by a human", because
"AI is very good at being overly verbose and including noise that
distracts from the main point." This is the clearest statement of the
problem the 2.2.0 rule addresses, from a project that is not anti-AI
(Ghostty is "written with plenty of AI assistance").

**Tejas Kumar, "Please Stop the Slop"** (10 October 2026; read through a
subagent's report, not directly). Lints his own prose with Vale and 39
custom rules, each written after catching a model writing a sentence in
his voice that he did not want. Useful sentence shapes he names, all of
which an existing rule here already covers:

- announcing that an answer exists before giving it ("has one big thing
  going for it:"), covered by "Don't announce a point before making it";
- an afterthought stapled on with a comma (", though."), covered by the
  trailing-clause tell;
- a last line that points back at the previous one and crowns it,
  covered by "Stop when the content stops";
- opening by handing the question back ("For a side project like
  yours,"), which no rule names. It is not added: it is one author's
  observation, unmeasured.

He also reports Graphite's phrase counts for Claude Opus 5.5 ("this
matters" at about 116 times the human rate). Those went into
`reference/claude-tics.md`, marked as secondhand.

His swap test ("Imagine the same comment under somebody else's post on
the same topic. Slop reads just as well there") is the writing form of
loupe's "would this fit any product?" and of this skill's Overview
sentence about choices that "would suit any reader and any subject".

**Jake Nations, "Vibe Coding Our Way to Disaster"**, **Dex Horthy, "Why
Software Factories Fail"** and **swyx, "No More Slop"** (all read through
the same report) are about code and agent workflow, not prose. Their
points are recorded in the global guidance file the author keeps
outside this repository, not here.

## 2. What slop costs the reader

**Workslop.** Niederhoffer, Kellerman, Lee, Liebscher, Rapuano and
Hancock, "AI-Generated 'Workslop' Is Destroying Productivity", *Harvard
Business Review*, 22 September 2025 (secondhand: the HBR page is
paywalled; figures from BetterUp's own write-up and press). In a survey
of US desk workers, 40% had received workslop in the past month, and
recipients reported spending close to two hours on each instance
(BetterUp says 1 h 51 min, most press 1 h 56 min). About half saw the
sender as less creative, capable and reliable. A vendor survey, not
peer-reviewed; the sample is given as 1,150 in one place and 1,004 in
another.

**The suspicion penalty is the best-replicated finding here.**

- Jakesch, French, Ma, Hancock and Naaman (CHI 2019; secondhand): Airbnb
  profiles labeled or suspected as AI-written were trusted less, but only
  when raters saw a mix of AI and human profiles.
- Hohenstein et al., *Scientific Reports* 13:5487 (2023; abstract): using
  smart replies made conversations faster and more positive, but people
  suspected of using them were rated more negatively.
- Reif, Larrick and Soll, *PNAS* 122 (2025; abstract): four preregistered
  experiments, N = 4,439. People who use AI at work are rated as less
  competent and less motivated.
- Schilke and Reimann, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision
  Processes* 188 (2025; abstract): across 13 experiments, disclosing AI
  use lowers trust, and being exposed by someone else lowers it more.

For this skill, that cuts two ways. Tells that make a reader suspect a
model cost the sender trust, which is the case for the whole skill.
And the skill must not be used to hide AI use the reader is owed:
`SKILL.md` already says it does not target detectors, and Schilke and
Reimann's exposure result is a practical reason as well as an ethical
one.

## 3. Why models write long

- Singhal, Goyal, Xu and Durrett, "A Long Way to Go: Investigating
  Length Correlations in RLHF", COLM 2024 (read). PPO roughly doubled
  output length on WebGPT (100 to 230 tokens). The authors attribute 70
  to 90% of the reward improvement on two of three datasets to length,
  and a reward for length alone came close to standard PPO.
- Dubois, Galambosi, Liang and Hashimoto, "Length-Controlled
  AlpacaEval", COLM 2024 (abstract): controlling for length raised the
  benchmark's correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98.
- Li, Angelopoulos and Chiang, LMSYS blog, August 2024 (read): in
  Chatbot Arena votes, length is the largest style effect (coefficient
  0.249), and markdown effects are second order.

So length is something training rewards, not a sign that the content
needed it. A rule that tells the writer to size the piece to the reader
is pushing against a known bias.

## 4. Plain words and short messages

- Oppenheimer, "Consequences of erudite vernacular utilized irrespective
  of necessity", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20 (2006; citation
  confirmed, findings secondhand): swapping words for their longest
  thesaurus synonym lowered readers' judgments of the author's
  intelligence, mediated by how easily the text was processed. This is
  direct support for "Use the plain verb and the common word", which
  until now rested on style guides.
- Kimble's surveys of Michigan judges and lawyers (secondhand): 80 to 85%
  preferred the plain-English version of a passage.
- Nielsen, "How Little Do Users Read?", NN/g, 2008 (read): on an average
  web page, users have time to read at most 28% of the words, about 20%
  in practice, and each extra 100 words adds about 4.4 seconds of
  attention.
- Rogers and Lasky-Fink, *Writing for Busy Readers* (2023; book excerpt
  read): in a field experiment with 7,002 school board members, a
  49-word email got a 4.8% response against 2.7% for a 127-word version
  asking for the same thing. Nearly everyone asked in advance predicted
  the longer email would do better.

**What was not found.** No controlled study shows that putting the
conclusion first (BLUF, the inverted pyramid) improves comprehension of
messages. Inverted-pyramid results in news are mixed. The nearest classic
support is Bransford and Johnson (1972), where giving readers the topic
before a passage raised comprehension and recall. The evidence for short
messages and scannable structure is stronger than the evidence for
answer-first order. That is why the 2.2.0 rule was tested before it was
adopted (`reference/held-out-reader-first-test/`) rather than added on
the literature alone.

## 5. Homogenization

- Doshi and Hauser, *Science Advances* 10 (2024; read): AI story ideas
  made individual stories more novel (up to 8.1%) and the set of stories
  more alike (by about 10% of the human range).
- Padmakumar and He, ICLR 2024 (read): only the instruction-tuned model
  reduced the diversity of essays written with it, and the loss came
  from the model's own text. Users' own text was unaffected.
- Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha, CHI 2025 (read): AI suggestions moved
  Indian writers' essays toward American style; a classifier's accuracy
  at telling the groups apart fell from 90.6% to 83.5%, and a Diwali
  essay lost its specific religious detail.

These are the measured version of the skill's Overview: text reads as
machine-written when its choices would suit any reader and any subject.
Agarwal et al. add a reason to keep the Scope rule about a person's own
voice, and to keep culture-specific detail when editing.

## 6. What changed in SKILL.md, and what did not

**Changed.** One rule, "Lead with what the reader came for", added in
2.2.0 after a pre-registered held-out test. All four conditions held.
Its second sentence, about leaving out facts the reader will not use,
had no measurable effect in that test; see `result.md` there.

**Considered and not changed.**

- A rule to disclose AI authorship. Whether to disclose is the sender's
  decision and depends on where the text goes. The skill writes the
  text, it does not send it.
- A length budget. The length literature supports cutting, but a number
  would be invented, and the skill promises none. The new rule sizes the
  piece to the reader instead.
- Tejas Kumar's "handing the question back" opener. One author's
  observation, unmeasured.
- Named commitments made on the user's behalf ("I'll confirm Thursday").
  The judges in the 2.2.0 test listed several of these as invention. The
  rule against inventing does not name them, and the October test of a
  wording that named a related kind of claim did not clear its bar. A
  future held-out test could target this directly.
