## 1. Probability-based detection: perplexity, log-likelihood, and curvature

### 1.1 Core claim: LLM text sits in lower-perplexity, higher-probability regions than human text

**Claim:** LLM-generated text tends to exhibit lower perplexity and more uniform, higher token-level probabilities under a language model than human-authored text, because generation is (approximately) a search through the model's own high-probability regions, while humans are not optimizing against any single model's probability distribution.

**Source:** This is the foundational assumption behind essentially the entire "zero-shot statistical detector" literature, most explicitly operationalized in:
- Mitchell, E., Lee, Y., Khazatsky, A., Manning, C. D., & Finn, C. (2023). *DetectGPT: Zero-Shot Machine-Generated Text Detection using Probability Curvature.* ICML 2023. arXiv:2301.11305. https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11305
- Gehrmann, S., Strobelt, H., & Rush, A. M. (2019). *GLTR: Statistical Detection and Visualization of Generated Text.* ACL 2019 (System Demonstrations). https://github.com/HendrikStrobelt/detecting-fake-text

**Credibility note:** DetectGPT is a peer-reviewed ICML paper from a Stanford NLP group (Manning, Finn are established, highly-cited researchers); it is one of the most-cited papers in this literature and has spawned a direct lineage of follow-up work (see below). GLTR is a joint MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab / Harvard NLP project, also peer-reviewed (ACL), and is one of the earliest systematic treatments of this idea (pre-dating ChatGPT, originally built around GPT-2).

**Consensus status:** Multi-source. This is corroborated independently by Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars, and multiple survey papers (see below), all of which build on the same underlying statistical premise using different estimators.

**Mechanism detail (DetectGPT specifically):** DetectGPT's specific contribution is not "low perplexity" per se but a more refined signal: *text sampled from an LLM tends to occupy negative curvature regions of the model's log-probability function*: small random perturbations of LLM-generated passages tend to have *lower* log-probability under the source model than the original, whereas perturbations of human text do not show this asymmetric drop. This is a more robust signal than raw perplexity because it doesn't require the passage to be unusually "predictable" in absolute terms, only unusually *locally optimal* relative to nearby paraphrases. DetectGPT requires only log probabilities from the model of interest plus perturbations generated by a separate, generic model (e.g., T5), with no classifier training or labeled data required.

**Reported quantitative result:** DetectGPT achieved 0.95 AUROC detecting fake news articles generated by a 20B-parameter GPT-NeoX model, versus 0.81 AUROC for the strongest zero-shot baseline (rank-based GLTR-style statistics), a substantial improvement attributed specifically to the curvature signal rather than raw perplexity/rank alone.

**Credibility note on the number:** Single benchmark, single paper: a meaningful but not yet independently replicated effect size; later papers (Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) report their own AUROCs on overlapping but not identical benchmarks, so exact numbers should be treated as method- and dataset-specific rather than universal constants.

### 1.2 Follow-up: conditional probability curvature is faster and more discriminative

**Claim:** Replacing DetectGPT's expensive perturbation-based curvature estimate with a "conditional probability curvature" computed via efficient sampling preserves (and modestly improves) detection accuracy while being roughly two orders of magnitude faster.

**Source:** Bao, G., Zhao, Y., Teng, Z., Yang, L., & Zhang, Y. (2024). *Fast-DetectGPT: Efficient Zero-Shot Detection of Machine-Generated Text via Conditional Probability Curvature.* ICLR 2024. arXiv:2310.05130. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05130

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed at ICLR (a top-tier ML venue), direct methodological successor to DetectGPT with the same evaluation lineage, making its comparative claims reasonably trustworthy within the zero-shot-detection sub-literature.

**Reported quantitative result:** Fast-DetectGPT reports roughly a 75% relative improvement in detection accuracy over DetectGPT in both white-box (model-known) and black-box (model-unknown) settings, at approximately 340x the speed (i.e., the perturbation step, which required many separate LLM calls, is replaced by a single efficient sampling step).

**Consensus status:** Single paper for the exact numbers, but the underlying idea (probability-curvature methods generalize and can be made cheaper) is consistent with the broader zero-shot detection literature's trajectory toward "compare a passage's likelihood under one model against its likelihood under a perturbed/resampled version" as the core signal.

### 1.3 Binoculars: contrasting two related models instead of perturbing text

**Claim:** Comparing the perplexity of a passage under one language model ("observer") against the *cross-perplexity* of that passage under a second, closely related model's predictions ("performer") yields a ratio that is a strong, domain-agnostic, zero-shot signal for human vs. machine authorship, without needing access to the specific model that generated the text and without training a classifier.

**Source:** Hans, A., Schwarzschild, A., Cherepanova, V., Kazemi, H., Saha, A., Goldblum, M., Geiping, J., & Goldstein, T. (2024). *Spotting LLMs With Binoculars: Zero-Shot Detection of Machine-Generated Text.* ICML 2024. arXiv:2401.12070. https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12070 (code: https://github.com/ahans30/Binoculars)

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed ICML 2024 paper; Tom Goldstein's lab (University of Maryland) is a recognized, highly active group in both LLM watermarking and detection research (Goldstein is also a co-author on the Kirchenbauer watermarking paper below), giving this cross-paper coherence in methodology and terminology.

**Reported quantitative result:** Binoculars detects over 90% of generated samples from ChatGPT (and other LLMs) at a false-positive rate of just 0.01%, across a wide range of document types, *despite never being trained on ChatGPT-specific data*: it generalizes zero-shot to a model family it wasn't calibrated on.

**Consensus status:** This specific number is single-source (one paper's benchmark), but it is broadly consistent with the general finding across this literature that statistical/zero-shot methods can achieve high accuracy against *non-adversarial* text (text not deliberately modified to evade detection), a qualifier that matters a great deal, per Section 4 below.

### 1.4 Combined perplexity + entropy features

**Claim:** A classifier combining perplexity and the entropy of the token-probability distribution as features achieves strong separation between human and machine text (reported AUC 0.96 in one study), with entropy of token probabilities (a proxy for the generating model's "predictive confidence") proving particularly discriminative on its own.

**Source:** Referenced in aggregate search results tied to "Perplexity and Token-Probability-Based Detection of Machine-Generated Text" (ResearchGate-indexed study); directly consistent with the broader GLTR framework (Gehrmann et al. 2019, above), whose three original statistical tests were explicitly (1) whether a generated word is sampled from the top of the model's predicted distribution, (2) its rank within that distribution, and (3) the entropy of the distribution itself (testing whether the model is "overconfident" given prior context).

**Credibility note:** The GLTR component of this claim is peer-reviewed and well-established; the specific AUC=0.96 figure comes from a less centrally verified secondary source and should be treated as illustrative of the general finding (entropy is informative) rather than a load-bearing precise statistic.

**Human-detection angle:** GLTR's original human-subject study found that showing people the visual "footprint" (color-coded token-rank overlay) raised the human detection rate of machine-generated text from 54% to 72% without any additional training, suggesting these statistical signals, when surfaced to humans, are more discriminative than humans' unaided intuition.

**Consensus status:** Multi-source for the general claim (perplexity/entropy/rank-based signals are discriminative); the specific human-improvement numbers (54%→72%) are single-source (GLTR paper only).

### 1.5 Nuance: bigger, more human-aligned models are harder to catch this way

**Claim:** The reliability of perplexity/probability-based detection is inversely related to model quality and alignment. Larger, more capable, RLHF-tuned models (e.g., GPT-3.5/4-class models) produce text that more closely resembles the perplexity profile of human writing and is correspondingly *harder* to separate via these statistical methods, whereas smaller or less-aligned models (e.g., XLNet, BLOOM-class) remain comparatively easy to distinguish.

**Source:** Aggregated finding surfaced across multiple secondary sources discussing the general perplexity-detection literature; directly consistent with the theoretical argument in Sadasivan et al. (Section 4.1) that detection difficulty scales inversely with model quality.

**Credibility note:** This is best treated as an emergent, multi-paper consensus rather than a single citable statistic. It is the qualitative trend reported across nearly every detection benchmark paper published from 2023 onward (detection AUROCs consistently degrade as the generator model is upgraded from GPT-2-class to GPT-3.5/4-class).

**Consensus status:** Multi-source, high confidence as a qualitative trend; not reducible to one number.

## 2. Watermarking research

### 2.1 The green-list/red-list watermark

**Claim:** It is possible to embed a statistically detectable but human-imperceptible "watermark" directly into LLM output at generation time, by pseudorandomly partitioning the vocabulary into "green" and "red" token lists (reseeded per-token based on preceding context) and softly biasing sampling toward green tokens. A downstream statistical test (without needing the model's weights or API access) can then detect the watermark from a short span of tokens with an interpretable p-value.

**Source:** Kirchenbauer, J., Geiping, J., Wen, Y., Katz, J., Miers, I., & Goldstein, T. (2023). *A Watermark for Large Language Models.* ICML 2023. arXiv:2301.10226. https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10226

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed ICML 2023 paper from a well-known group (University of Maryland, Tom Goldstein again; Jonathan Katz is a leading cryptographer, lending rigor to the statistical-test / security framing). This is widely regarded as the paper that kicked off the modern LLM watermarking sub-field and has an extensive citation lineage (semantic-invariant watermarks, black-box watermarks, cross-task watermarking frameworks, etc., several of which turned up directly in search results as 2024-2026 follow-ups).

**Important scope note:** This is *not* a claim about naturally occurring, unwatermarked LLM text having detectable statistical fingerprints. It is a claim about a deliberately engineered intervention at generation time. It is relevant to the broader question only insofar as it establishes (a) that LLM providers *can* deliberately bias token selection to create a fingerprint if they choose to, and (b) by construction/contrast, that *without* such an intervention, no equivalent guaranteed statistical signal exists: detection of unwatermarked text has to fall back on the weaker, probabilistic signals in Section 1.

**Consensus status:** Single foundational paper, but its core mechanism (biased green/red token sampling) is now a broad multi-paper sub-literature (semantic-invariant watermarks, robust cross-task watermarking, black-box watermarking, etc.), indicating the core approach is considered sound and extensible by the community, even though real-world deployment by major LLM providers remains limited/inconsistent.

### 2.2 Watermarks are not fully robust to adversarial paraphrasing

**Claim:** Even watermarked text can have its watermark washed out through paraphrasing attacks, and — more troublingly — attackers can in some cases infer or approximate a hidden watermark's signature through querying, which creates a spoofing risk (generating non-watermarked text that is falsely flagged as watermarked, damaging the credibility of the underlying model/provider).

**Source:** Sadasivan, V. S., Kumar, A., Balasubramanian, S., Wang, W., & Feizi, S. (2023, rev. 2025). *Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?* TMLR (Transactions on Machine Learning Research). arXiv:2303.11156. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156

**Credibility note:** Published in TMLR, a peer-reviewed, rolling-review ML venue with a reputation for rigor; Soheil Feizi's lab (University of Maryland) is an established adversarial-robustness research group. This paper is widely cited as the primary theoretical counterweight to the optimism of the detection literature.

**Consensus status:** This is directly corroborated by watermarking follow-up papers that exist specifically to shore up robustness (e.g., "A Semantic Invariant Robust Watermark for Large Language Models," arXiv:2310.06356, and "A Watermark for Black-Box Language Models," arXiv:2410.02099). The very existence of a robustness-focused sub-literature is evidence the original vulnerability claim is taken seriously and considered valid by the field. Multi-source consensus.

## 3. Stylometric / linguistic-feature research (non-probability-based)

This is the area most directly relevant to human-perceptible "tells" rather than model-internal statistics, and is generally more directly transferable to a writing/humanizing context than the probability-curvature literature above.

### 3.1 Contrasting linguistic patterns in human vs. LLM news text (large, carefully controlled study)

**Claim:** Across many independent linguistic dimensions (lexical richness, sentence-length variance, syntactic constituent variety, dependency-length optimization, part-of-speech distribution, and even emotional tone), human-written news text differs systematically and consistently from LLM-generated news text, and these human–LLM differences are larger than the differences *between* different LLMs.

**Source:** Muñoz-Ortiz, A., Gómez-Rodríguez, C., & Vilares, D. (2024). *Contrasting Linguistic Patterns in Human and LLM-Generated News Text.* Artificial Intelligence Review (Springer), published online August 23, 2024. DOI: 10.1007/s10462-024-10903-2. Also on PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11422446/

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed journal article in a Springer-indexed venue (Artificial Intelligence Review), from a Spanish NLP group (Universidade da Coruña) known for syntactic/dependency-parsing work. Methodologically careful: the authors specifically used New York Times articles published *after* the training cutoffs of the six LLMs tested (Mistral 7B, Falcon 7B, LLaMA 7B/13B/30B/65B), explicitly to avoid the confound of the models having memorized similar articles. This is one of the more methodologically rigorous studies in this list.

**Specific findings (all from this one paper, so treat internally consistent but externally single-source unless cross-referenced below):**
- **Lexical diversity:** Human texts had higher vocabulary richness: Standardized Type-Token Ratio (STTR) of 0.491 for humans vs. a range of 0.424–0.466 across the six LLMs. LLaMA models came closest to human-level diversity; Falcon was furthest.
- **Sentence length variance:** Humans produced more varied sentence lengths; LLMs concentrated their output heavily in the 10–30 token range, i.e., a narrower, more centrally clustered distribution, a direct, methodologically grounded instance of the informally-named "burstiness" phenomenon (see 3.2 below).
- **Part-of-speech patterns:** LLM text contained more numbers, symbols, and auxiliary verbs (suggestive of a more "objective"-sounding register); human text used more nouns, adjectives, and punctuation; LLMs relied comparatively more on pronouns.
- **Dependency-length optimization:** Human sentences achieved *better* dependency-length optimization (i.e., syntactically more efficient, shorter-effective-distance structures) despite having longer absolute dependency lengths. The authors interpret this as reflecting real constraints of human working memory that LLMs do not replicate the same way.
- **Constituent variety:** LLMs overused verb phrases and subordinate clauses and underused noun and prepositional phrases, while showing less overall constituent-type variety than humans.
- **Emotional tone:** Human text expressed stronger negative emotions (fear, disgust); LLM text skewed toward more "joy." The paper also reports that model toxicity increased with model size.
- **Gender bias:** Human text already showed a 1.71:1 male-to-female pronoun ratio; most LLMs (all except Falcon) *exacerbated* this imbalance, with LLaMA models increasing the ratio by roughly 15%.

**Consensus status:** The sentence-length-variance and lexical-diversity findings are corroborated by multiple other papers below (3.2, 3.3, 3.5), making those two sub-claims multi-source. The dependency-optimization, POS-distribution, emotional-tone, and gender-bias findings are, in this specific quantified form, single-source (this paper only) among the sources gathered here, though they are qualitatively consistent with the general "LLMs are more syntactically uniform/templatic" theme running through the rest of this survey.

### 3.2 "Burstiness" and sentence-complexity variance as a detection heuristic

**Claim:** Human writing shows high "burstiness" (substantial variation in sentence complexity and rare-word usage from sentence to sentence), while LLM output is comparatively uniform across sentences, keeping complexity/rarity metrics in a narrower band throughout a passage. This is one of the most commonly cited heuristics in applied/informal AI-detection tooling (e.g., GPTZero-style tools), and it does have a basis in the peer-reviewed literature.

**Source (peer-reviewed corroboration):** The sentence-length-variance component of this claim is directly measured and confirmed in Muñoz-Ortiz et al. (2024), above (Section 3.1) and echoed in Herbold et al. (2023) and Przystalski et al. (2025) below.

**Credibility note:** "Burstiness" as a named, formalized construct is more associated with applied AI-detection products (GPTZero and similar) than with a single canonical peer-reviewed paper defining it under that name; however, the underlying statistical phenomenon it describes — variance in sentence length and lexical rarity — is independently measured and confirmed by at least three separate academic sources in this survey. Treat the *name* "burstiness" as informal/product-originated but the *underlying measured phenomenon* as multi-source-supported.

**Consensus status:** Multi-source for the underlying phenomenon (sentence-length/complexity variance is lower in LLM text); single-source/informal for the specific "burstiness" framing and terminology.

### 3.3 Stylometry recognizes human vs. LLM text even in short samples

**Claim:** Classical stylometric feature sets (lexical, grammatical, syntactic, and punctuation-level n-gram and hand-designed markers, e.g., via the StyloMetrix toolkit) can reliably distinguish human from LLM-generated text even at short lengths (10-sentence samples), and can further distinguish *pure* LLM output from LLM-summarized or LLM-rephrased human content, a harder, finer-grained task than plain human-vs-machine binary classification.

**Source:** Przystalski, K., Argasiński, J. K., Grabska-Gradzińska, I., & Ochab, J. K. (2025). *Stylometry recognizes human and LLM-generated texts in short samples.* arXiv:2507.00838. https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00838

**Credibility note:** arXiv preprint (2025) from a group whose methodology draws on StyloMetrix, an established stylometric-analysis toolkit; the study design (multiclass classification across 7 categories including different summarization methods, not just a binary human/AI split) is more granular than most detection papers, which strengthens the specificity of its conclusions but also means some numbers below are from a single, not-yet-widely-cross-validated study.

**Reported quantitative results:**
- 7-category multiclass classification: Matthews correlation coefficient of 0.87.
- Binary classification accuracy ranged 0.79–1.0 depending on category pairing, with Wikipedia-vs-GPT-4 reaching 0.98 on balanced datasets.
- The paper explicitly characterizes LLM output as showing "greater grammatical standardisation" than human writers, along with characteristic word preferences and encyclopedic-register conventions (their dataset was Wikipedia-based).

**Consensus status:** The general finding ("stylometric features remain discriminative, feature-based classifiers outperform perplexity-only baselines") is corroborated in aggregate by a related, independently-titled study, "Feature-Based Detection of AI-Generated Text: An Analysis of Stylometric and Perplexity Markers in Contemporary Large Language Models" (ResearchGate-indexed), which reports a combined feature set (stylometric + perplexity) reaching F1 = 0.94, "significantly outperforming" perplexity-only models, directionally consistent, though the exact numbers are from separate, not-fully-verified studies and should not be treated as identical replications.

### 3.4 Essays: ChatGPT text has fewer discourse/epistemic markers, more nominalization, higher lexical diversity, and is rated *higher* quality by human graders

**Claim:** In a large-scale, controlled comparison of argumentative essays, ChatGPT-generated essays were rated as *higher quality* than human-written essays by expert human graders (teachers), despite (or alongside) exhibiting distinct, systematically different linguistic patterns: fewer discourse and epistemic markers, more nominalizations, and greater lexical diversity than the human essays in that specific dataset.

**Source:** Herbold, S., Hautli-Janisz, A., Heuer, U., Kikteva, Z., & Trautsch, A. (2023). *AI, write an essay for me: A large-scale comparison of human-written versus ChatGPT-generated essays.* arXiv:2304.14276. https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14276

**Credibility note:** arXiv preprint, but a large-scale, methodologically explicit study using real human expert graders (not just automated metrics) as the quality benchmark, a stronger design than most detection papers, which typically only measure classifier accuracy rather than also measuring perceived writing *quality*. Widely cited in subsequent detection and AI-in-education literature.

**Important tension flagged:** This paper's lexical-diversity finding (LLM text is *more* diverse) directly **contradicts** the Muñoz-Ortiz et al. (2024) finding (LLM text is *less* diverse, lower STTR) and the Kendro et al. (2025, Section 3.5 below) finding (ChatGPT text diverges from human diversity patterns, and newer/larger models diverge *more*, not less). This is an explicit case of **conflicting findings across studies** and should be flagged as such rather than resolved by picking a side: lexical-diversity comparisons appear highly sensitive to genre (news vs. argumentative essays vs. Wikipedia-style text), model version, and the specific diversity metric used (simple type-token ratio vs. multi-dimensional diversity measures). Treat "LLMs are less lexically diverse than humans" as a plausible-but-contested claim, not settled multi-source consensus, despite appearing in more of the sources gathered here than the contrary claim.

**Consensus status:** Mixed/contested, flagged explicitly. The discourse-marker and nominalization findings, by contrast, are not directly contradicted elsewhere in this survey and align with the general "LLM text is more syntactically/structurally uniform and formal-register" theme.

### 3.5 Multi-dimensional lexical diversity: LLMs diverge from human patterns, and newer models diverge *more*

**Claim:** Across six independent dimensions of lexical diversity (volume, abundance, variety-repetition, evenness, disparity, and dispersion, a more granular framework than simple type-token ratio), ChatGPT-generated text differs significantly from human-written text on every dimension measured, and — counterintuitively — newer/larger ChatGPT versions (o4-mini, GPT-4.5) diverge *more* from human patterns than older versions, with GPT-4.5 producing the least human-like lexical-diversity profile despite the comparison group being deliberately broad (240 human writers spanning L1 and L2 English speakers across multiple education levels, who were themselves highly *consistent* in diversity regardless of background).

**Source:** Kendro, K., Maloney, J., & Jarvis, S. (2025). *Do LLMs produce texts with "human-like" lexical diversity?* arXiv:2508.00086. https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00086

**Credibility note:** 2025 arXiv preprint; notable for using a validated, multi-dimensional lexical-diversity framework (attributed to Jarvis's own prior work on lexical diversity measurement, a recognized specialty within applied linguistics) rather than a single crude metric, and for a relatively large, demographically varied human comparison sample (240 writers). The "newer models are less human-like on this axis" finding is counterintuitive relative to the general trend elsewhere in this survey (larger/more aligned models are usually harder to distinguish via perplexity; see 1.5) and is worth flagging explicitly as a nuance: general detectability and lexical-diversity-matching are not the same axis, and a model can become harder to catch on one dimension (probability curvature) while becoming easier to catch on another (diversity-pattern mismatch).

**Consensus status:** Single-source for the specific "newer models diverge more" finding; broadly consistent with (rather than contradicting) the *general direction* of Muñoz-Ortiz et al.'s finding that LLM text has lower vocabulary richness than human text, even though the two studies use different metrics and don't directly cross-validate each other.

### 3.6 Coherence, cohesion, and discourse-marker usage differ, but not in the "more markers = more coherent" direction one might assume

**Claim:** (a) LLM-generated text tends to follow more templatic patterns and diverges from human text on coherence-related measures such as coreference tracking; (b) AI text tends to use more conjuncts (contributing to more segmented sentence structure) while human text uses more prepositional phrases (contributing to a more relational, contextually embedded structure); (c) counterintuitively, across general writing-quality research, a *higher* frequency of explicit discourse markers/connectives does not straightforwardly correlate with higher perceived logical coherence: one study found a negative correlation between logic ratings and discourse-marker frequency, and prior work similarly found no correlation between cohesion-index usage (e.g., discourse connectives) and high vs. low essay proficiency.

**Source:** Aggregated from search-indexed discussion of "Differentiating Between Human-Written and AI-Generated Texts" (arXiv:2407.03646) and related discourse-coherence literature (e.g., DiscoScore, arXiv:2201.11176, for coherence-evaluation methodology) surfaced in this research pass.

**Credibility note:** This cluster of claims is weaker-sourced than the rest of this document. It comes from secondary aggregation of search results rather than a single fully-read, clearly-authored primary paper, so treat the coreference/conjunct/prepositional-phrase claims as suggestive rather than firmly established, pending direct verification of the primary source (arXiv:2407.03646, not independently fetched and read in full during this research pass).

**Consensus status:** The general "explicit discourse-marker density does not equal perceived coherence" point is a genuinely interesting counter-intuitive finding worth flagging, but it should be treated as tentative/single-cluster-sourced within this survey.

### 3.7 Novel: human narrative/creative writing is more diverse than LLM output at the plot level

**Claim:** Beyond sentence- and word-level stylometry, human-written stories show greater structural and thematic diversity than LLM-generated stories at the level of plot and narrative structure. LLMs tend to converge on a narrower set of recurring plot patterns ("echoes") across many independent generations, even under different prompts.

**Source:** *Echoes in AI: Quantifying lack of plot diversity in LLM outputs.* PNAS. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2504966122 (also indexed at PMC12415252)

**Credibility note:** Published in PNAS (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences), a highly prestigious, rigorously peer-reviewed multidisciplinary journal, one of the strongest-venue sources in this survey, though it addresses creative/narrative generation specifically rather than general prose style, so its applicability to non-narrative writing (essays, articles, technical writing) is not established and shouldn't be over-generalized.

**Consensus status:** Single source in this survey, but high-credibility venue; directionally consistent with the general "LLMs are more uniform/templatic than humans" theme running through nearly every other source gathered here (sentence length, lexical diversity, syntactic constituent variety, and now narrative structure all point the same direction: convergence/uniformity in LLM output, variance/burstiness in human output).

## 4. Fundamental limits, adversarial robustness, and the "arms race" framing

### 4.1 Theoretical impossibility result: detection accuracy degrades toward chance as models improve

**Claim:** For a sufficiently capable language model whose output distribution approaches the human text distribution in total variation distance, even the *best possible* detector (not just current ones) can perform only marginally better than random guessing. This is a formal result, not merely an empirical observation: the paper derives a relationship between the AUROC of an optimal detector and the total variation distance between the human and AI text distributions, showing detection reliability is fundamentally bounded by how distinguishable the two distributions actually are, a bound that shrinks as models improve. The paper additionally demonstrates empirically, via a "recursive paraphrasing attack," that this isn't just theoretical: paraphrasing substantially degrades real detector performance (watermarking, neural classifiers, zero-shot methods, and retrieval-based detectors alike) while preserving much of the original text's quality/meaning. The paper further shows that watermark detection schemes are vulnerable to spoofing: an attacker can approximate a watermark's signature without full system access, which creates a reputational risk for LLM providers (someone could generate harmful non-watermarked text engineered to be falsely flagged as watermarked and attributed to the provider).

**Source:** Sadasivan, V. S., Kumar, A., Balasubramanian, S., Wang, W., & Feizi, S. (2023, rev. through Jan 2025). *Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?* TMLR. arXiv:2303.11156. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed, TMLR (rolling peer review, well-regarded in the ML community for rigor); combines a formal theoretical result with a concrete empirical attack, which is unusual rigor for this literature and is why this paper is widely treated as the central counter-argument to detection-optimism in the field. Revised as recently as January 2025, indicating ongoing relevance/engagement rather than a stale one-off preprint.

**Consensus status:** The empirical "paraphrasing evades most detectors" finding is corroborated by the very existence of RADAR (below) and other adversarially-robust detector proposals, which were built specifically as a response to this vulnerability: the field's response constitutes indirect multi-source validation that the vulnerability is real and taken seriously. The formal impossibility-as-models-improve argument is more theoretical and, while influential, remains a single paper's framing (it has been debated, not universally accepted as the final word: later papers like Binoculars report very high accuracy numbers against *non-adversarial*, i.e., non-paraphrased, current-generation model output, which is not strictly a contradiction, since Sadasivan et al.'s claim is specifically about the adversarial/paraphrase-attack setting and the asymptotic limit as models keep improving, not about today's off-the-shelf, non-adversarial text).

### 4.2 Adversarially-robust detection as a direct response

**Claim:** Standard AI-text detectors (neural classifiers in particular) are not robust to LLM-based paraphrasing attacks, but robustness can be substantially improved by explicitly training the detector *against* an adversarial paraphraser in a joint minimax setup: the paraphraser learns to generate realistic paraphrases that evade the detector, and the detector learns from that adversarial signal, iteratively.

**Source:** Hu, X., Chen, P.-Y., & Ho, T.-Y. (2023). *RADAR: Robust AI-Text Detection via Adversarial Learning.* NeurIPS 2023. arXiv:2307.03838. https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03838

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed NeurIPS 2023 paper (poster); Pin-Yu Chen (IBM Research) is a well-established adversarial-ML researcher, adding credibility to the adversarial-training methodology specifically.

**Reported result:** Evaluated across 8 different LLMs (Pythia, Dolly 2.0/1.0, Palmyra, Camel, GPT-J, LLaMA, Vicuna) and 4 datasets, RADAR significantly outperforms prior detection methods specifically under paraphrasing attack conditions, and the paper reports strong transferability from instruction-tuned LLMs to other LLM families it wasn't specifically trained against.

**Consensus status:** This paper functions as direct, practical corroboration of Sadasivan et al.'s vulnerability claim (Section 4.1) — its entire premise is built on that vulnerability being real — making the "paraphrasing evades naive detectors" claim multi-source/high-confidence, even though the exact robustness numbers RADAR reports are single-paper.

### 4.3 Humans are poor at this task unaided, and improving detection methods have optimized for fooling humans specifically

**Claim:** (a) Non-expert human evaluators, without training, distinguish GPT-3-authored text from human text at essentially random-chance accuracy across multiple domains (stories, news articles, recipes); with structured training (detailed instructions, annotated examples, paired comparisons), accuracy improves but only to around 55%, barely better than chance, and does not differ much across writing domains. (b) Separately and more subtly: improvements in decoding/sampling methods for text generation have been optimized empirically to fool *human* raters specifically, and these same improvements often introduce statistical abnormalities that make the text *easier*, not harder, for automatic statistical detectors to catch: "what fools humans" and "what fools statistical detectors" are not the same target, and optimizing for one can work against the other. Even so, well-crafted multi-sentence excerpts fooled expert human raters over 30% of the time in that study.

**Sources:**
- Clark, E., August, T., Serrano, S., Haduong, N., Gururangan, S., & Smith, N. A. (2021). *All That's 'Human' Is Not Gold: Evaluating Human Evaluation of Generated Text.* ACL-IJCNLP 2021 (Long Papers), pp. 7282–7296. arXiv:2107.00061. https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.565/
- Ippolito, D., Duckworth, D., Callison-Burch, C., & Eck, D. (2020). *Automatic Detection of Generated Text is Easiest when Humans are Fooled.* ACL 2020. arXiv:1911.00650. https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.164/

**Credibility note:** Both are peer-reviewed ACL papers (top-tier NLP venue) from well-known authors: Noah A. Smith (University of Washington / Allen Institute for AI) is a leading NLP researcher; Chris Callison-Burch (UPenn) is likewise well established. These are foundational, frequently cited papers in the human-evaluation-of-generated-text sub-literature and predate the ChatGPT era (using GPT-2/GPT-3), meaning their core methodological point (humans are weak, unaided detectors, and detector optimization targets diverge from human-fooling optimization targets) has had years to be stress-tested and is widely accepted, even as the specific accuracy numbers are naturally dated relative to today's much stronger models.

**Consensus status:** Multi-source, and this pairing of papers is itself a well-known and frequently-cited "pair" in the field precisely because they make complementary, mutually reinforcing points (one about humans' absolute weakness, the other about the humans/statistics divergence). High confidence as a qualitative finding: humans are not reliable unaided AI-text detectors, and refinements to LLM decoding tend to specifically target and erode human-detection ability, sometimes independently of (or even at odds with) statistical detectability.

### 4.4 A caveat to the "humans are bad at this" claim: frequent LLM users may be a partial exception

**Claim:** People who frequently use ChatGPT for writing tasks in their own lives may be more accurate and more robust human detectors of AI-generated text than the general population, potentially relying on different or more refined cues than infrequent users.

**Source:** *People who frequently use ChatGPT for writing tasks are accurate and robust detectors of AI-generated text.* arXiv:2501.15654 (2025). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15654

**Credibility note:** Recent (2025) arXiv preprint; this research pass was not able to fully extract the paper's text (PDF parsing failed for this source), so authorship, venue, and precise methodology could not be independently verified beyond the title and topic as surfaced by search. **This finding should be treated as provisional and lower-confidence pending direct verification** — it's included because it's a directly relevant, recent, and specific counterpoint to the Clark et al. / Ippolito et al. "humans are poor detectors" consensus, but it is single-source and not independently confirmed within this survey.

**Consensus status:** Single source, unverified in full text, flagged explicitly as the weakest-sourced claim in this document.

### 4.5 A survey-level framing of the whole field

**Claim:** The AI-text-detection literature can be organized into four broad families of method: (1) watermarking (proactive, generation-time intervention), (2) statistical/zero-shot methods (perplexity, curvature, rank/entropy-based, requiring no training), (3) trained neural classifiers (supervised, requiring labeled human/AI text datasets), and (4) retrieval-based methods (checking whether a passage or close paraphrase of it exists in a database of known LLM outputs), each with distinct strengths, weaknesses, and vulnerability profiles, and the field broadly acknowledges there is no single method that is simultaneously accurate, robust to paraphrasing, generalizable across model families, and computationally cheap.

**Source:** *Towards Possibilities & Impossibilities of AI-generated Text Detection: A Survey.* arXiv:2310.15264. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.15264

**Credibility note:** This research pass located and searched for this survey but could not extract its full text (PDF parsing failed), so its specific taxonomy details, authorship, and conclusions are reconstructed here from the paper's title and its consistent framing with every other individually-verified paper in this document (each of the four categories named above is independently confirmed by at least one directly-read paper elsewhere in this survey: watermarking by Kirchenbauer et al., statistical/zero-shot by DetectGPT/Fast-DetectGPT/Binoculars/GLTR, neural classifiers by the RoBERTa/GPT-2-detector work below, and adversarial robustness concerns by Sadasivan et al./RADAR). Treat the four-way taxonomy itself as reliable (it's corroborated structurally by the rest of this document) but treat any specific claim attributed only to this survey as unverified.

## 5. Trained classifier baselines

### 5.1 The original OpenAI/HuggingFace GPT-2 detector

**Claim:** A RoBERTa-large model (355M parameters) fine-tuned specifically to discriminate GPT-2-generated text (particularly from the 1.5B-parameter GPT-2 model) from human "WebText" achieved 83–85% accuracy identifying text generated with top-k=40 sampling, an early, foundational example of the supervised-classifier approach to detection, released by OpenAI alongside their staged release of GPT-2 as part of a broader responsible-release/detection-research strategy.

**Source:** OpenAI / HuggingFace collaboration; model card at https://huggingface.co/openai-community/roberta-large-openai-detector (RoBERTa architecture originally from Liu et al., Facebook AI, 2019).

**Credibility note:** This is an industry-released model/tool rather than a traditional peer-reviewed paper, but it comes from OpenAI directly (the GPT-2 developer) as part of their documented staged-release research process, and the RoBERTa base architecture itself is peer-reviewed (Liu et al. 2019). It is widely cited as a baseline in nearly every subsequent detection paper, giving it strong indirect validation even though the original release write-up is a technical report/blog rather than a conference paper.

**Consensus status:** Treated as an accepted baseline/reference point across the field (every subsequent detection paper in this literature benchmarks against classifiers of this general RoBERTa-classifier type), but the specific 83–85% figure is dated (GPT-2-era) and not informative about detecting current-generation (GPT-4/Claude/Gemini-class) models, which produce qualitatively different, harder-to-classify text.

## 6. Lexical-marker / "excess vocabulary" research (word-choice fingerprints)

This line of research is distinct from stylometric/statistical detection: instead of trying to classify a document as human or AI, it measures *population-level* shifts in specific word frequencies across a large corpus (e.g., all PubMed abstracts) before and after LLMs became widely available, as indirect evidence of LLM-assisted writing at scale.

### 6.1 Excess use of specific words in biomedical scientific writing since ChatGPT

**Claim:** Certain words and phrases — "delve," "underscore," "meticulous," "commendable," "intricate," and others — showed dramatic, statistically anomalous increases in usage frequency in biomedical abstracts specifically coinciding with and following ChatGPT's public release, at a rate and scale inconsistent with normal year-over-year vocabulary drift. Applying an "excess mortality"-style statistical framework (borrowed from epidemiology, but applied to word-usage rates) to a corpus of over 15 million PubMed abstracts from 2010–2024, researchers estimated that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts showed evidence of LLM-assisted writing, with the figure reaching as high as 40% in some sub-corpora (by discipline, country, or journal), an effect described as larger than the measurable impact of major world events such as the COVID-19 pandemic on scientific-writing vocabulary.

**Source:** Kobak, D., González-Márquez, R., et al. *Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary.* Science Advances (2025), DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.adt3813. Preprint: arXiv:2406.07016. https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016 / https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813

**Credibility note:** Published in *Science Advances*, a peer-reviewed, high-impact-factor journal from the American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS), one of the strongest venues cited in this entire survey. Dmitry Kobak (University of Tübingen) is an established computational biology/statistics researcher; the "excess mortality" methodological analogy is a clever, statistically principled way to separate ordinary vocabulary drift from an anomalous LLM-driven shift, since it uses each word's own pre-2023 trend as its individual counterfactual baseline rather than assuming a flat baseline. Methodologically one of the more rigorous papers in this survey.

**Consensus status:** Corroborated independently by a separate PubMed-focused study (below) using a related but distinct methodology, and by a dedicated follow-up study on the *causes* of this phenomenon (also below). This is genuinely multi-source, high-confidence territory.

### 6.2 Independent PubMed corroboration, with an important caveat about pre-existing trends

**Claim:** A separate analysis of PubMed records confirmed that terms associated with LLM usage ("delve" chief among them) have drastically changed in frequency since ChatGPT's arrival — but this study adds an important nuance: usage rates for several of these words were *already increasing* for multiple years *before* ChatGPT's release, suggesting ChatGPT (and LLMs generally) likely accelerated a pre-existing linguistic trend in scientific English rather than single-handedly originating it from a flat baseline.

**Source:** *Delving into PubMed Records: Some Terms in Medical Writing Have Drastically Changed after the Arrival of ChatGPT.* medRxiv (preprint), 2024. https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.05.14.24307373v2.full (v1 independently confirmed during this project's citation audit; the v2 revision is referenced here but wasn't independently re-confirmed)

**Credibility note:** medRxiv preprint (not yet peer-reviewed at time of indexing): should be weighted slightly lower than the Science Advances paper above, but it is methodologically independent (different research group, overlapping but not identical corpus/timeframe) and its core "acceleration of a pre-existing trend, not pure origination" nuance is an important qualifier that the Kobak et al. paper's framing (which emphasizes the sharp post-ChatGPT jump) doesn't fully surface, worth preserving as a genuine methodological caveat rather than a flat contradiction.

**Consensus status:** Multi-source for "these specific words spiked post-ChatGPT"; the "already rising pre-ChatGPT" nuance is presently single-source among the sources reviewed here, and somewhat in tension with (though not strictly contradicting, since "accelerated a rising trend" is compatible with "spiked further") the framing of the Science Advances paper.

### 6.3 Why does this happen? RLHF as a candidate mechanism, but not a fully confirmed one

**Claim:** Researchers systematically tested multiple competing hypotheses for *why* ChatGPT and similar LLMs overuse a specific set of words (they identified 21 such "focal words," including "delve," "intricate," and "underscore," selected because their frequency growth in scientific English is anomalous and largely absent from other corpora like plain arXiv text, Wikipedia, or general news outside of LLM-influenced contexts). They tested and largely ruled out training-data overrepresentation, model architecture, and generic algorithmic/decoding choices as primary causes. Comparing Llama 2-Base (no RLHF) against Llama 2-Chat (with RLHF) suggested that reinforcement learning from human feedback specifically contributes to this lexical overrepresentation (i.e., the fine-tuning/alignment stage, not the base pretraining, appears to be where these word preferences are most amplified), though a follow-up exploratory human-preference study aimed at pinning down exactly *why* human raters (whose preferences shape RLHF) might favor these words was inconclusive. Notably, participants in that human-preference study showed marked wariness specifically toward "delve" appearing in opening sentences, suggesting public meta-awareness of the "AI tell" may itself now be starting to feed back into future training data and preference signals.

**Source:** Juzek, T. S., & Ward, Z. B. (2024/2025). *Why Does ChatGPT "Delve" So Much? Exploring the Sources of Lexical Overrepresentation in Large Language Models.* COLING 2025. arXiv:2412.11385. https://arxiv.org/html/2412.11385v1 / https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.426.pdf

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed at COLING 2025 (a major computational linguistics conference), authored by Florida State University linguists (Tom Juzek, Zina Ward) with a philosophy-of-science-informed hypothesis-elimination methodology (explicitly testing and ruling out multiple competing explanations rather than asserting one) — a methodologically careful piece of work, and per FSU's own news coverage, described as "the first scientific evidence pointing toward why ChatGPT tends to overuse certain words," i.e., it's explicitly positioned by its own authors as the first paper to move this question from anecdote to controlled hypothesis-testing.

**Consensus status:** Single source for the RLHF-causal-mechanism finding specifically (this is the first and, per the authors' own framing, only paper directly testing *why*), though it builds on and is consistent with the multi-source empirical observation (6.1, 6.2) that the *phenomenon* itself (word-frequency spikes) is real and robust. The causal mechanism (RLHF) should be treated as a well-argued but not yet independently replicated explanation.

## 7. Repetition, degeneration, and distributional-divergence metrics

### 7.1 Neural text degeneration under naive decoding, and the motivation for nucleus sampling

**Claim:** Maximization-based decoding strategies (e.g., beam search, greedy decoding) applied to neural language models produce text that is bland, generic, or falls into repetitive loops — a phenomenon the authors term "neural text degeneration." This is a foundational, structural fact about how neural language models generate text at all (not specific to any one model), and it directly motivated the development of nucleus (top-p) sampling as an alternative decoding strategy that samples from the dynamic "nucleus" of the probability distribution (the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds a threshold p) rather than always taking the single most likely token.

**Source:** Holtzman, A., Buys, J., Du, L., Forbes, M., & Choi, Y. (2020). *The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration.* ICLR 2020. arXiv:1904.09751. https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed ICLR 2020 paper; Yejin Choi (University of Washington / Allen Institute for AI) is one of the most prominent NLP researchers working on text generation and is a co-author on many foundational papers in this space (including MAUVE, below). This is a canonical, extremely widely cited paper (foundational to essentially all modern LLM sampling/decoding configuration) and directly explains *why* naive/greedy LLM output would be maximally repetitive and detectable, and why virtually all deployed LLMs today use some form of nucleus or temperature sampling specifically to avoid this failure mode, which is itself relevant context: much of the "LLM text is repetitive/degenerate" folk intuition is based on decoding strategies that production systems have specifically engineered around since 2019.

**Consensus status:** This is foundational, essentially uncontested "textbook" NLP knowledge at this point (extremely high citation count, direct architectural influence on nearly all subsequent generation systems) — as close to settled consensus as anything in this document.

### 7.2 Repetition and lexical-diversity measurement tools, and mixed model-specific findings

**Claim:** (a) A standardized toolkit combining compression ratio, self-repetition of long n-grams, Self-BLEU, and BERTScore is sufficient to characterize text diversity/repetition, as these measures have low mutual correlation and each capture a distinct facet of repetitiveness. (b) Empirical findings on repetition are model-specific and inconsistent across the LLM landscape: essays written with InstructGPT showed higher repetition of common 5-grams than human essays and reduced lexical diversity, while separately, comparative testing found Gemini showed substantial "degeneration" (22.3% of samples containing 10 or more repeating 4-grams) whereas Claude showed none in the same test, indicating repetition/degeneration is not a universal property of "LLM text" as a category but varies significantly by specific model and likely by that model's specific decoding configuration and training regime.

**Source:** *Standardizing the Measurement of Text Diversity: A Tool and a Comparative Analysis of Scores.* arXiv:2403.00553. https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00553v1 (tool/metric paper); model-specific repetition findings (InstructGPT 5-gram repetition, Gemini/Claude 4-gram degeneration comparison) aggregated from search-indexed secondary discussion of this and related benchmarking work.

**Credibility note:** The diversity-measurement toolkit paper appears methodologically sound (explicitly validated via low inter-metric correlation, a reasonable design choice) but was not fetched and read in full text during this research pass. Treat the tool's existence and general approach as reasonably well-supported, but the specific InstructGPT/Gemini/Claude comparative numbers as secondary-sourced and lower-confidence, pending direct verification.

**Consensus status:** The *general* point (that repetition and lexical diversity should be measured with multiple complementary metrics because they capture different things, and that repetition behavior varies meaningfully by specific model rather than being a fixed "LLM" property) is a reasonable and important nuance for any downstream synthesis to preserve, but the specific percentage figures here are weaker-sourced (single aggregated secondary source) than most of the rest of this document.

### 7.3 MAUVE: distributional-gap measurement between human and machine text

**Claim:** Comparing the *overall distribution* of machine-generated text against human text (rather than classifying individual documents) using a divergence-frontier-based metric reveals systematic gaps that scale predictably with model size (larger/better models produce distributions closer to human text) and correlate with human quality judgments — providing a distributional, rather than per-document-classification, lens on the human/LLM text gap.

**Source:** Pillutla, K., Swayamdipta, S., Zellers, R., Thickstun, J., Welleck, S., Choi, Y., & Harchaoui, Z. (2021). *MAUVE: Measuring the Gap Between Neural Text and Human Text using Divergence Frontiers.* NeurIPS 2021 (Oral). arXiv:2102.01454. https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01454

**Credibility note:** Peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2021 *oral* presentation (a distinction indicating unusually strong peer reception; orals are a small fraction of accepted papers), from a strong multi-institution author list including Yejin Choi again. This paper is widely used as a standard *evaluation metric* in subsequent text-generation research (not just detection research specifically), giving it broad indirect validation through adoption.

**Consensus status:** The "gap scales with model size, better models are closer to human distribution" finding is directionally consistent with, and mutually reinforcing of, the Section 1.5 finding that larger/more aligned models are harder to catch via probability-based detectors. Both point to the same underlying trend of frontier models converging toward the human text distribution, even though MAUVE and DetectGPT-style curvature detection are measuring different things (aggregate distributional similarity vs. per-document local statistical anomaly). Multi-source-consistent as a directional trend, though the two families of metric are not literally measuring the same quantity.
